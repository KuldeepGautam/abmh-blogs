What is Valvular Heart Disease?

The valvular heart disease definition refers to disorders affecting the heart’s four valves, the mitral, aortic, tricuspid, and pulmonary valves. These valves open and close in a coordinated way to maintain proper blood circulation.

When a valve fails to open fully or close properly, blood flow becomes disrupted. It may affect circulation, blood pressure, and overall heart function.

How Heart Valves Work?

The heart has four valves that act like one-way doors, ensuring blood flows in the correct direction:

  • The mitral valve controls blood flow between the left atrium and left ventricle
  • The aortic valve directs oxygen-rich blood to the body
  • The tricuspid valve regulates blood flow on the right side of the heart
  • The pulmonary valve sends blood to the lungs

Healthy valve function helps maintain a stable heart rate and efficient oxygen delivery. When valves are damaged, narrowed (stenosis), or leaking (regurgitation), the heart must compensate, leading to increased strain.

Types of Valve Disorders

Valve disorders are classified into three main categories:

  • Valve Stenosis: The valve opening becomes narrow, restricting blood flow.
  • Valve Regurgitation: The valve does not close completely, allowing blood to flow backwards.
  • Valve Prolapse: The valve flaps bulge backwards, disrupting normal circulation.

Each type varies in severity and requires tailored management.

Causes of Valvular Heart Disease

There are several recognised causes of valvular heart disease, including:

  • Age-related wear and tear
  • Congenital valve abnormalities
  • Rheumatic fever
  • Infections such as endocarditis
  • High blood pressure
  • Damage from previous heart disease
  • Autoimmune conditions

Lifestyle and existing cardiovascular conditions may increase risk over time.

Symptoms of Valvular Heart Disease

Symptoms may develop gradually and differ depending on the affected valve:

  • Shortness of breath
  • Fatigue or reduced stamina
  • Chest discomfort
  • Swelling in the ankles or legs
  • Dizziness or fainting
  • Rapid or irregular heart rate
  • Difficulty with physical activity

Some individuals may not experience noticeable symptoms until the condition progresses.

Diagnosis

Diagnosing valvular heart disease involves a detailed clinical assessment and specialised testing:

  • Echocardiogram: primary test to assess valve structure and function
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG): evaluates heart rhythm
  • Chest X-ray: identifies heart enlargement
  • Cardiac MRI or CT scan: provides detailed imaging
  • Exercise or stress testing evaluates heart performance

Monitoring blood pressure and rhythm patterns helps determine the impact of disease.

Treatment Options for Valvular Heart Disease

Modern treatment of valvular heart disease focuses on relieving symptoms, protecting heart function, and preventing complications.

Medications: Help control symptoms, regulate blood pressure, and stabilise heart rhythm.

Lifestyle Adjustments: Heart-friendly habits support overall cardiovascular health.

Valve Repair or Replacement: Surgical or minimally invasive procedures restore valve function.

Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator (ICD): In some cases, an Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator may be recommended to manage dangerous heart rhythm disturbances.

Treatment plans are personalised based on severity, age, and overall health.

FAQs on Valvular Heart Disease:

1. Is valvular heart disease serious?

If left untreated, it can become serious, but early diagnosis and appropriate treatment help most patients manage symptoms and maintain good heart health.

2. Can valvular heart disease be treated without surgery?

Yes. Mild cases are often managed with medication, monitoring, and lifestyle changes, while more severe valve problems may require procedural treatment.

3. When is surgery needed for valve disease?

Surgery is considered when valve damage significantly affects heart function, causes symptoms, or increases the risk of long-term complications.

4. Can lifestyle change improve valve health?

Healthy habits like regular exercise, a balanced diet, and blood pressure control support heart function and may slow disease progression.

5. When should I see a cardiologist?

Consult a cardiologist if you notice breathlessness, fatigue, chest discomfort, or irregular heartbeat, especially if symptoms persist or worsen.
